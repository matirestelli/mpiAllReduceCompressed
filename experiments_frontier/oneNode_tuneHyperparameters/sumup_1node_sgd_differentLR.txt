-> FINAL DECISION: 0.01

Is LR=0.2 the winner? Technically yes, but read this first
0.2 has the highest single number (90.17%) and the highest mean (89.7%). But look at the spread across 0.05 / 0.1 / 0.2: 89.2, 88.2, 89.7. That's a 1.5-point band, and your built-in-vs-ring gap within a single LR is up to 2 points (0.1: 87.20 vs 89.17) at identical hyperparameters.
That second fact is the important one. Built-in and ring are the same math — the 2-point gap between them at LR=0.1 is pure seed/initialization noise (plus fp32 non-associativity). So your measurement noise floor is ~±1–2 points. And 0.05, 0.1, 0.2 all sit inside that band. Statistically, they're tied. 0.2 "winning" by 0.5 points over 0.05 is within the noise you just measured.
So the honest reading isn't "0.2 is best" — it's "0.05–0.2 are a plateau; 0.01 is slightly below; 0.001 is broken." Any of the three plateau values is defensible.
Which to actually pick as LR_REF
I'd take 0.1, not 0.2, for a reason that only matters because of what comes next:
LR_REF gets multiplied by B_global/128 across your whole scaling study. At weak-scaling W3, P=64, that's LR_REF × 64:

LR_REF=0.1 → LR = 6.4
LR_REF=0.2 → LR = 12.8

Both are large-batch territory, but 12.8 is deep into "linear scaling has broken, expect divergence" — you'll be fighting instability in the exact rows that are hardest to interpret. Picking the lower end of the tied plateau buys you headroom at high P for free, with no accuracy cost at B_global=128 (0.1 and 0.2 are indistinguishable there). 0.05 would give even more headroom but sits at the edge where 0.01 starts dropping, so 0.1 is the safe center.
LR_REF = 0.1. Tied for best at the anchor, most robust when scaled up.
